Most boardrooms are still asking "how many seats can AI take out?"

That leaves money on the table, and Torsten Slok just laid out why.

His read on the Jevons effect for AI is the most contrarian — and most useful — labor framing I have seen. The short version: cheaper "thinking work" creates more thinking work, not less. Efficiency multiplies demand. It's the same story as the steam engine and coal in the 1860s; it'll be the story of GenAI and analyst-hours in the 2020s.

I've started running it as a thought experiment in every PortCo conversation:

"If I dropped 10,000 college graduates onto your team tomorrow — free, fully ramped — what business problem would you finally tackle?"

Most people I ask need a minute. A few have a 4-second answer — usually the ones who've already made an AI bet. Most pause and surface a list of three things they've been meaning to get to. A handful realize, mid-sentence, that they don't actually know yet.

All three answers are useful. The 4-second answers tell you who's already executing. The pauses tell you where the next 24 months of DPI is going to come from.

A real example from last quarter. An NBA basketball team needed to systematically scout players — scaling without dramatically growing labor operations. A computer vision pipeline took care of that. Now the team has all the data and systematic, structured reports on all players and can spend their time deciding on the best fit instead, empowered by ready-made individual player assessments.

The world didn't shrink. The level the work got done at went up.

That's Jevons in the field. AI didn't replace the scout — it deleted the part of the job any disciplined intern could do, and gave them ten times the prospect pool to evaluate.

If you own a portco in healthcare, business services, manufacturing, financial services, or logistics, there's a version of this hiding in your business right now.

The right framing isn't "where can we cut?" It's "where are we letting expensive talent do $25 work?"
