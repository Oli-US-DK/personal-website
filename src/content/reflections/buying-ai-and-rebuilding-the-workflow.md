Bain just published a finding I haven't been able to stop thinking about: 40% of companies that measured AI cost savings landed below 10% — despite targeting 11–20%. The tech worked. The value didn't.

Bain's diagnosis — workflow debt, data access, operating model redesign — is right. But there's a sharper version of it underneath, and it explains what I see in new client conversations every week:

In the first wave of AI, most companies tried to buy AI the way they buy SaaS. SaaS economics reward point solutions: one tool, one workflow step, predictable license fee, fast deployment. That model works when you're digitizing a sub-process. It breaks when the goal is workflow and business transformation — because "AI for meeting notes" doesn't change how the meeting drives a downstream decision. It just adds token cost (visible, or hidden in a SaaS bundle) without changing the economics of the workflow it's bolted onto.

The companies in Bain's outperforming cohort pick one business problem, fix the workflow first, unify the structured and unstructured data their humans were manually stitching together, then automate each step with deterministic logic where it fits, agentic logic where appropriate, and humans in the loop where the stakes demand it. Then they instrument it with real evals so token consumption gets managed like any other unit economic.

That's the difference between "I bought an AI tool" and "I rebuilt a business process where AI sits inside the architecture, not bolted on top of it." Bain frames this as organizational. It is — but the technical and organizational sides aren't separable. The winners aren't just changing how the organization operates; they're changing the nature of the software they buy.

And that's where the compounding kicks in. Once a company gets one workflow right, the value capture funds the next workflow, and the next, until the whole operating model has been re-architected. That's not AI adoption. That's a different kind of company.
