Imagine that every one of your customers suddenly has the equivalent of 10,000 people available to evaluate suppliers. They can run more tenders, ask more detailed questions, and revisit the quotes they already have. How quickly could your business respond?

There are two familiar ways to think about a new capability like this. One is to ask what your own team could finally do with it. The second is to ask what happens if a competitor gets it first. The customer version is easier to miss. It changes what counts as a good response even if your offering is as strong as it was yesterday.

## The buyer can do more with the same team

Consider a small consumer goods business looking for a new supplier. Its sourcing operation can pull quotes from several countries, read offer sheets, and compare options before asking a person to make the final call.

Consider what that does to the economics of a search. A buyer with a fixed amount of time might have tested three suppliers and gone with the best available offer. If comparing an extra twenty suppliers becomes cheap, the buyer can ask a narrower question and look further for a better answer. They can also afford to reject a quote they would once have worked around.

A single buyer changing how it works might be invisible from the supplier’s side. Several buyers doing it at once could generate more enquiries, more follow-up questions, and more unusual combinations of requirements. Each request still looks like an enquiry. Taken together, they test a commercial process designed for a different pace.

## Where a supplier feels it first

A buyer asks for a quote that includes a volume discount, an alternative delivery window, and the cost of changing a specification. Someone in sales checks the price list, someone in operations confirms capacity, and a manager signs off on the exception. Each person can do their part well and the answer can still arrive after the buyer has moved on.

The constraint in that example is the path from the question to a dependable answer. A faster email response helps very little if key facts sit in different systems or every exception waits for the same person. It can also backfire if the customer receives a quick commitment the supplier cannot honor.

I’d look first at the time from request to a usable answer, especially for requests involving exceptions. Track how long each handoff takes and whether the customer drops out before receiving the quote. Break the data down by request type. An overall average can look healthy while the requests most likely to turn into valuable business are getting slower.

## Where speed matters less

Some buying decisions still hinge on relationships, regulatory requirements, or the ability to deliver reliably over years. A buyer may happily wait for a specialist to answer a difficult question. And when enquiries are easy to generate, some of the extra volume will come from buyers who were never likely to transact.

That makes qualification more valuable. A supplier should know which answers can come from reliable product and pricing data, which should go to a person, and which requests deserve more time because the commercial stakes justify it. The aim is to make routine answers easy to deliver and give specialists enough time to handle the decisions customers hire them for.

## A question for the next commercial review

Take one category where a good prospect can already compare several suppliers. Trace a real quote from the first enquiry to a decision. Where does the customer wait? What information does the team have to reconstruct? Which approvals change the decision, and which could be governed by rules?

Then ask what would happen if qualified enquiries doubled while buyers expected answers in half the time. That is a useful stress test even if neither number becomes reality. It shows whether the business can serve a more capable customer and gives the team a concrete place to start.
